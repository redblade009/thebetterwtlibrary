Nature has set up four kinds of barriers to man's conquest of the earth—seventieth, forests, deserts and rivers. The first he cannot remove, so he boxes holes through them for railways. The second he has, most unwieldy, largely cleared away altogether. The third he is beginning to treat like the forests. The fourth he is shifting to suit his purposes and to regulate their flow at will.

Man flies more over all boundaries. He cuts through lethamess to remove the barriers between the seas.

Into the deserts man sends railroads, telegraph lines, irrigation engineers. The "Great American Desert" marked upon the atlases of our fathers has ceased to exist. The vast "desert" of southwestern Canada has become a boundless prairie of waving wheat. The Landes of Glencony are now much more than half covered with pine trees. In 1902, 22,000 square miles of the Algerian desert had been made fruitful by artesian wells. The "Australian desert" is rapidly being irrigated and turned into grazing land. Almost 70,000 square miles of "Desert" in India has been reclaimed. Sir William Wilcox is now engaged in reclaiming 10,000 square miles of the delta of the Tigris and Euphrates and more than 4,000 square miles of the Gesires plain between the Blue and White Nile are being transferred into cotton plantations.

Thus is man, by obliterating natural barriers, improving upon nature—Exchange.

For the world to have known of the Divine purposes in advance would doubtless have been injurious, because in their blindness they would have attempted to thwart these purposes and therefore would have gotten themselves into a worse condition of condemnation.
